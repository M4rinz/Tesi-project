\chapter{Matrix Functions}\label{capitolo:2}
This chapter introduces the matrix functions, their definitions, their basic properties and their conditioning. It aims at laying the ground for the presentation that will be given in Chapter~\ref{capitolo:3} of the matrix exponential, the matrix function this essay focuses on, therefore the treatment will be concise, giving multiple specific references for further details.

\section{Introduction}
The general idea behind the functions of a matrix is that a function $f\colon\Sigma\subseteq\C\to\C$ is extended to its counterpart on square matrices, $f\colon\Omega\subseteq\C{n}{n}\to\C{n}{n}$, in such a way that the core properties are preserved.
This is \emph{very} different from applying (the scalar) $f$ to the elements of $A\in\C{n}{n}$.

\begin{esempio*}
	By analogy with the complex exponential, the matrix exponential function $\exp\colon\C{n}{n}\to\C{n}{n}$ should map the zero matrix to the identity matrix.  
	Instead, the element-wise application of the complex exponential, denoted -- using Julia notation -- by $\exp\!.$, returns $\exp\!.(0_{n\times n})=\mathrm{e}\,\mathrm{e}^{\!\top}$, where $\mathrm{e}\in\C{n}$ is the vector of all ones.
\end{esempio*}

The simplest possible matrix function is the polynomial of a matrix. This example hardly deserves a more abstract treatment, as many properties can be directly proven. Another simple example is the solution $x_0$ to a nonsingular linear system $Ax=b$ ($A\in\C{n}{n}$, $b\in\C{n}$), which can be expressed as $x_0=f(A)b$ where $f(z)=z^{-1}$.

More sophisticated examples of matrix functions include the matrix sign, the matrix square root, the matrix logarithm and the matrix exponential. Matrix functions have broad applications in fields such as control theory and network analysis; see~\cite[§~2]{higham:matfun_book} for furhter examples and a broader discussion of their possible applications. 

We anticipate that, in order to be \emph{defined} on square matrices, $f$ must satisfy some additional properties that may not be necessary for the definition of the original scalar function $f$. Specifically, the existence of derivatives at certain points must be guaranteed, up to a certain order.

\section{Definitions of matrix function}\label{sect:matfun_defs}
Matrix functions can be defined in three equivalent ways. This section will introduce only the two that will be used throughout all this essay. Even though it can lead to short proofs of many theoretical results, the Cauchy integral definition will not be treated here. It can be found in~\cite[§~1.2.3]{higham:matfun_book} 

\subsection*{Jordan form definition}
The first definition we introduce makes use of the Jordan form of $A$.

\begin{notazione*}
	Let $\mathbb{C}\supset\hat{\Lambda}(A)=\qty{\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_s}$ be the \emph{spectrum} of $A$ with the convention that the eigenvalues are repeated with \emph{geometric multiplicity}. 
\end{notazione*}
Then $A$ can be written in \emph{Jordan canonical form} as
\begin{equation}\label{eqn:jordan_A}
	A = V\,\operatorname{Blkdiag}\qty(J_{n_1}(\lambda_1),\dots,J_{n_s}(\lambda_s))\,V^{-1}
\end{equation}
where each $J_{n_k}(\lambda_k)$ is a \emph{Jordan block}, of the form 
\[
	J(\lambda_k)\coloneqq
	\begin{pNiceMatrix}[nullify-dots]
		\lambda_k & 1      &        & \\
		& \Ddots & \Ddots & \\
		&        &        & 1\\
		&        &        & \lambda_k\\                    
	\end{pNiceMatrix}\in\C{n_k}{n_k}.
\]
We recall that each of the $\mu_g(\lambda_k)$ ``copies'' of $\lambda_k$ is tied to an invariant subspace of size $n_k$. This invariant subspace is generated by a \emph{Jordan chain}, i.e. a set of \emph{generalized eigenvectors} $\qty{v_1,\dots,v_{n_k}}$ such that $v_j=(A-\lambda_kI)v_{j+1}\ (j\in[n_k-1])$ and $v_{n_k}\in\ker(A-\lambda_kI)^{n_k}\setminus\ker(A-\lambda_kI)^{n_k-1}$. 

We recall that the \emph{generalized eigenspace} of $\lambda_k$ is given by the direct sum of the aforementioned invariant subspaces, or equivalently, by 
\[
	\qty{v\in\mathbb{C}^n\mid \exists\,r\in\mathbb{N}\quad(A-\lambda_k I)^rv=0}\equiv\ker(A-\lambda_k I)^{n(\lambda_k)}
\]
where $n(\lambda_k)$ is the \emph{index} of $\lambda_k$, i.e. the size of the largest Jordan block in which $\lambda_k$ appears, or equivalently the multiplicity of $\lambda_k$ as a root of the minimal polynomial of $A$.
\begin{oss}\label{oss:jordan_unique}
	Recall that the Jordan canonical form of $A$ is unique up to reordering of the blocks $J_{n_k}$; in particular, the block dimensions $n_1,\dots,n_k$ are unique. However, the change of basis $V$ is not unique. 
\end{oss}
More details on the Jordan canonical form can be found in~\cite[§~3]{horn_johnson_2012:book}. 

\noindent In order to be able to define $f$ on $A$ as in~\eqref{eqn:jordan_A}, the condition in the Definition~\ref{def:f_def_spectrum} needs to hold.
\begin{definizione}\label{def:f_def_spectrum}
	The function $f\colon\Sigma\subseteq\C\to\C$ is said to be \emph{defined on the spectrum of} $A$ if it is differentiable at least $n_k-1$ times on $\lambda_k$ for each $\lambda_k\in\hat{\Lambda}(A)$.
	
	Equivalently, $f$ is defined on the spectrum of $A$ if the values $f^{(j)}(\lambda)$ exist for all $j=0,\dots,n(\lambda)-1$, for all eigenvalues $\lambda\in\Lambda(A)$.
\end{definizione}

We can finally give the definition of matrix function via Jordan form.
\begin{definizione}\label{def:matfun_Jordan}
	Let $A\in\C{n}{n}$ have the Jordan canonical form~\eqref{eqn:jordan_A}, let $f$ be defined on the spectrum of $A$.
	Then $f$ is \emph{defined} on $A$, and
	\begin{equation*}
		f(A)\coloneqq V\,\operatorname{Blkdiag}\big(f(J_{n_1}(\lambda_1)),\dots,f(J_{n_k}(\lambda_k))\big)\, V^{-1}
	\end{equation*}
	where 
	\begin{equation}\label{eqn:f_Jordan_block}
		f\qty(J_{n_k}(\lambda_k))\coloneqq
		\begin{pNiceMatrix}[nullify-dots]
			f(\lambda_k) & f'(\lambda_k) & \Ldots & & \frac{f^{(n_k-1)}(\lambda_k)}{(n_k-1)!} \\
					     & f(\lambda_k)  & \Ddots & & \Vdots \\
						 & 		  		 & \Ddots & & \\
						 &        		 &        & & f'(\lambda_k)\\
						 &        		 &        & & f(\lambda_k) \\                    
		\end{pNiceMatrix}\in\C{n_k}{n_k}.
	\end{equation}
\end{definizione}
Definition~\ref{def:matfun_Jordan} comes natural if $f$ is a polynomial, in fact~\eqref{eqn:f_Jordan_block} can be proven by a simple computation.

In light of Remark~\ref{oss:jordan_unique}, it may be objected that Definition~\ref{def:matfun_Jordan} is ill-posed, because it depends on the change of basis $V$. It turns out that $f(A)$ can be defined in alternative ways that don't make use of $V$, and that these definitions are equivalent to Definition~\ref{def:matfun_Jordan}. 

\subsection*{Polynomial interpolation definition}
The second definition of matrix function is based on polynomial interpolation.

\begin{definizione}\label{def:matfun_poly}
	Let $f$ be defined on the spectrum of $A\in\C{n}{n}$, let $p\in\C\qty[t]$, with $\deg{p}<\sum_{\lambda_k\in\hat{\Lambda}(A)}n_k=n$ be a polynomial that interpolates $f$ and its derivatives on $\hat{\Lambda}(A)$, i.e. such that 
	\begin{equation}\label{eqn:interp_conditions}
		f^{(j)}(\lambda_k)=p^{(j)}(\lambda_k)\qquad\forall\,j=0,\dots,n_k-1\quad\forall\,\lambda_k\in\hat{\Lambda}(A).
	\end{equation}
	Then $f$ is \emph{defined} on $A$, and $f(A)\coloneqq p(A)$.
\end{definizione}
It can be proven that such a polynomial can always be constructed.

Some of the conditions in~\eqref{eqn:interp_conditions} are repeated if in $\hat{\Lambda}(A)$ there are repeated eigenvalues. However, the interpolation conditions relative to each ``copy'' of a repeated eigenvalue are consistent. Thus, they may be satisfied by a polynomial of degree lower than $n-1$. 

Notice that a polynomial $p\in\mathbb{C}[t]$ satisfies~\eqref{eqn:interp_conditions} if and only if, for each eigenvalue $\lambda\in\Lambda(A)$, $p$ interpolates $f$ on $\lambda$ up to the $\qty(n(\lambda)-1)$\emph{th} derivative. This requirement can thus replace~\eqref{eqn:interp_conditions} in Definition~\ref{def:matfun_poly}, together with $\deg{p}<\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda(A)}n(\lambda)$. There is only one polynomial of minimal degree, known as the \emph{Hermite polynomial}, that satisfies these conditions. There are explicit formulas for this polynomial, see also~\cite[§~1.2.2]{higham:matfun_book}.

It can easily be proven that, for $p,q\in\C\qty[t]$, $p(A)=q(A)$ if and only if $p$ and $q$ coincide on the spectrum of $A$ (with algebraic multiplicity). See~\cite[Thm.~1.3]{higham:matfun_book} for details. Therefore if $p$ and $q$ are polynomials that satisfy~\eqref{eqn:interp_conditions} then $f(A)=p(A)=q(A)$. So a polynomial of higher degree than necessary can nevertheless produce $f(A)$. A higher degree than necessary may be the result of imposing more interpolation conditions.

As a result, Definition~\ref{def:matfun_poly} is well posed, as it doesn't depend on the choice of the polynomial $p$ satisfying the interpolation conditions. 

\begin{oss*}
	In Definition~\ref{def:matfun_poly} no change of basis appears, but $f(A)$ is defined as a polynomial in $A$. This \emph{doesn't} mean that the matrix function itself is a polynomial, i.e. that there exists a $q\in\C\qty[t]$ such that $f(A)=q(A)$ for all $A\in\C{n}{n}$ for which $f(A)$ is defined. In fact, in Definition~\ref{def:matfun_poly}, $p$ depends on $A$.
\end{oss*}

\section{Basic properties of matrix functions}
Many properties follow from the definition of matrix function. Some of the most useful ones will be listed here, for further readings one can refer to~\cite[§~1.3]{higham:matfun_book}. Some specific properties of the matrix exponential are listed in Section~\vref{sect:expm_def_properties}.

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:matfun_trivial_properties}
	Let $A\in\C{n}{n}$ and let $f$ be a function defined on the spectrum of $A$. The following properties hold:
	\begin{enumerate}
		\item $Af(A)=f(A)A$
		\item $f(MAM^{-1})=	Mf(A)M^{-1}$
		\item If $A=\operatorname{Blkdiag}\qty(A_{11},\dots,A_{mm})$ is block diagonal, \\
			  then $f(A)=\operatorname{Blkdiag}\qty(f(A_{11}),\dots,f(A_{mm}))$.
		\item $\Lambda(f(A))=\qty{f(\lambda)\mid\lambda\in\Lambda(A)}$, $f(A)$ and $A$ have the same generalized eigenvectors.
	\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
	All the properties are easy consequences of the Jordan canonical form definition, or the polynomial interpolation definition. For a full proof, see~\cite[Thm.~1.13]{higham:matfun_book}, where more properties are listed.
\end{proof}

\begin{oss*}
	The fourth property of Lemma~\ref{lemma:matfun_trivial_properties} estabilishes a close relationship between the eigenvalues of $A$ and those of $f(A)$. In particular, by looking at $f(J_{n_k})$ for each of the Jordan blocks in the canonical form of $A$~\eqref{eqn:jordan_A}, one can see that the algebraic multiplicities are the same, i.e. $\mu_a(\lambda)=\mu_a(f(\lambda))\quad\forall\,\lambda\in\Lambda(A)$.
	
	However, the Jordan structure itself may be radically different. The subspaces' sizes $n_k$ may be different, in particular $\mu_g(\lambda)=\mu_g(f(\lambda))$ does not hold in general.
	
	The full situation is described in~\cite[Thm.~1.36]{higham:matfun_book}, to which the reader is referred for further details.
\end{oss*}

\begin{lemma}
	Let $f$ and $g$ be functions defined on the spectrum of $A\in\C{n}{n}$. The following properties hold:
	\begin{enumerate}
		\item Let $h(z)=f(z)+g(z)$. Then $h(A)=f(A)+g(A)$
		\item Let $h(z)=f(z)\cdot g(z)$. Then $h(A)=f(A)\cdot g(A)$
		\item If $g$ is $n_k-1$ times differentiable on $f(\lambda_k)$ for each $\lambda_k\in\hat{\Lambda}(A)$, then $h=g\circ f$ is defined on the spectrum of $A$, and $h(A) = g(f(A))$
	\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
	The first two properties follow from the polynomial interpolation definition of matrix function, see~\cite[Thm.~1.15]{higham:matfun_book}. The full proof of the third property -- which still follows from the polynomial interpolation definition, but is slighly more involved -- can be found in~\cite[Thm.~1.17]{higham:matfun_book}.
\end{proof}

The property in Lemma~\ref{lemma:matfun_f_continuity} trivially follows from the Jordan block definition of matrix function, but has important consequences.
\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:matfun_f_continuity}
	Let $\qty{f_m}_{m\in\mathbb{N}}$ be a sequence of functions defined on the spectrum of $A\in\C{n}{n}$, such that, for a function $f$, 
	\[
	\lim_m f_m^{(j)}(\lambda_k)=f^{(j)}(\lambda_k)\quad\forall\,j=0,\dots,n_k\qquad\forall\,\lambda_k\in\hat{\Lambda}(A).
	\]
	Then $\lim_m f_m(A)=f(A)$. 
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
	By definition, $f_m$ is applied block by block to the Jordan form of $A$. Therefore we can assume without loss of generality that the Jordan form of $A$ consists in a single Jordan block $J$; there exists $V\in\C{n}{n}$ such that $A=VJV^{-1}$. Thus,
	\[
	\lim_m f_m(A)=\lim_m f_m(VJV^{-1})=\lim_m Vf_m(J)V^{-1}=V\,\lim_m f_m(J)\,V^{-1}.
	\]
	From~\eqref{eqn:f_Jordan_block} it follows that, under our assumptions, $\lim_m f_m(A)=f(A)$, which completes the proof.
\end{proof}
\begin{corollario_lemma}\label{cor:matfun_power_series}
	Let $f$ be defined by the power series expansion $f(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}c_k(z-\alpha)^k$ with radius of convergence $r>0$. If $A\in\C{n}{n}$ is such that $\rho(A-\alpha I)<r$ then
	\[
		f(A)=\lim_d\sum_{k=0}^{d}c_k(A-\alpha I)^k.
	\]
\end{corollario_lemma}
The assumption $\rho(A-\alpha I)<r$ can also be loosened to $\rho(A-\alpha I)\le r$, paired with the differentiability assumption of Lemma~\ref{lemma:matfun_f_continuity} when an eigenvalue lies on the border of the disk of convergence. See also~\cite[Thm.~4.7]{higham:matfun_book}.
\smallskip 

\noindent Lemma~\ref{lemma:matfun_A_continuity} states that, under appropriate assumptions (which are satisfied, in the complex case, if the function is holomorphic), matrix functions are continuous functions.
\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:matfun_A_continuity}
	Let $\Sigma$ be an open subset of $\R$ or $\C$, let $f$ be $n-1$ times differentiable on $\Sigma$. Then $\lim_{m}f(A_m)=f(A)$ for any sequence $\qty{A_m}_{m\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq\qty{A\in\C{n}{n}\mid\Lambda(A)\subseteq\Omega}$ such that $A_m\xlongrightarrow{m\to\infty}A$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
	The proof can be found in~\cite[Thm.~1.19]{higham:matfun_book} and the references therein. Note that the property can't be proven as a straightforward outcome of the polynomial interpolation definition; i.e. $\lim_{m}f(A_m)=\lim_{m}p(A_m)=p(\lim_{m}A_m)$ is \emph{not} a valid proof, because the interpolating polynomial $p$ depends on $A_m$.
\end{proof}
\smallskip 

Let $A\in\C{2}{2}$ be a $2$-by-$2$ matrix. One interpolating polynomial $p(t)$
such that $p(A)=f(A)$ is given by Netwon's backward difference formula, namely 
$p(t) = f[\lambda_1] + f[\lambda_1,\lambda_2](t-\lambda_1)$, where $\lambda_1,\lambda_2$ are 
the eigenvalues of $A$ (not necessarily distinct) and $f[\lambda_1,\lambda_2]$ denotes 
the \emph{divided difference}, defined as 
\begin{equation}\label{eqn:def_divided_difference}
	f\qty[\lambda_1,\lambda_2]\coloneqq
	\begin{dcases}
		\frac{f(\lambda_1)- f(\lambda_2)}{\lambda_1 - \lambda_2} & \lambda_1\ne\lambda_2\\
		f'(\lambda_1) & \lambda_1=\lambda_2.
	\end{dcases}
\end{equation}
Thus,
\begin{equation}\label{eqn:matfun_2by2_divdiff}
	f(A) = f(\lambda_1)I + f\qty[\lambda_1,\lambda_2](A-\lambda_1I).
\end{equation}
One immediate consequence of Equation~\eqref{eqn:matfun_2by2_divdiff} is that if 
$A=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}a & b \\  & d\end{smallmatrix}\right)$ is upper triangular, 
$f(A)$ can be easily written as 
\begin{equation}\label{eqn:matfun_2by2_tri}
	f\begin{pmatrix}
		a & b\\ & d
	\end{pmatrix}=
	\begin{pmatrix}
		f(a) & b f[a,d]\\ 
			 & f(d)
	\end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eqn:matfun_2by2_tri} serves as a building block of many algorithms for computing functions of upper triangular matrices.
For example, it is used in~\cite{higham:alhi09_new, higham:hf19_mpexpm} to improve the final phases of the algorithms, albeit in a slightly different form (see Equation~\eqref{eqn:expm_2by2_tri}). This will be discussed more in detail in Section~\ref{subs:sas_limitations_impr}.

\begin{oss*}
	Computing $f\smqty(a & b\\ & d)$ using~\ref{eqn:matfun_2by2_tri} is not particularly problematic if $a=d$, provided that a stable method for computing the derivative of $f$ is available.
	
	The problem arises when $a-d$ is small but not negligible. In this case, a na\"ive evaluation of the divided difference~\eqref{eqn:def_divided_difference} may lead to a larger relative error than necessary. 	
	For instance, the computation of the divided difference of the exponential function will be reformulated to avoid subtractive cancellation in Remark~\vref{oss:expm_2by2_tri}.
\end{oss*}


\section{Conditioning of matrix functions}\label{sect:cond_matfun}
The general definitions of absolute and relative condition numbers have been given in Definition~\ref{def:cond_number}, in Section~\ref{sect:conditioning_general}. Apart from choosing appropriate norms, nothing prevents us from applying those same definitions to matrix functions. 

In that same section we discussed explicit formulas for the condition number of vector-valued functions that involve the norm of the Jacobian matrix. More practical formulas would be welcome even in the case at hand.
To obtain it, a reasonable notion of derivative is needed. To this end, we introduce the \emph{Fréchet derivative} of a matrix function.

\begin{definizione}\label{def:frechet_derivative}
	The \emph{Fréchet differential} (often also called \emph{Fréchet derivative}) of  $f\colon\C{n}{n}\to\C{n}{n}$ at the point $A\in\C{n}{n}$ is a linear mapping 
	\[
		\begin{array}{rccl}
			L_f(A)\colon & \C{n}{n} & \longrightarrow & \C{n}{n}\\
					& E & \longmapsto & L_f(A)[E]
		\end{array}
	\]
	such that $f(A+E) = f(A) + L_f(A)[E] + o(\norm{E})$ for all $E\in\C{n}{n}$.
\end{definizione}
The definition of Fréchet differential can be generalized to functions between Banach spaces. For the purposes of this essay, Definition~\ref{def:frechet_derivative} is sufficient.

The Fréchet derivative of $f$ at $A$ may not exist, but if it exists then it is unique.  

\begin{notazione*}
	The notation $L_{f}(A)[E]$ can be read as ``the (Fréchet) derivative of $f$, in $A$, applied to $E$'', or ``the (Fréchet) derivative of $f$, computed in $A$, along the direction $E$''. 
	$L(A)$ denotes the linear operator $E\mapsto L(A)[E]$.
	
	%\textcolor{red}{Forse introdurre notazione $\dd f(A)$?}
\end{notazione*}

\begin{oss}\label{oss:frechet_generalizza}
	Notice that if, in Definition~\ref{def:frechet_derivative}, the function $f$ had different domain and range, the usual notions of derivative known from real analysis would be obtained. Namely:
	\begin{itemize}
		\item For $f\colon\R\to\R$, $L_f(x_0)[x]=f'(x_0)\,x$, i.e. $L_f(x)=f'(x)\dd{x}$
		\item For $f\colon\R{n}\to\R$, $L_f(x_0)[x]=\left\langle\grad{f}(x_0),x\right\rangle$, i.e. $L_f(x_0)\!=\sum_{i=1}^n\!\pdv{f(x_0)}{x_i}\dd{x_i}$
		\item For $f\colon\R{n}\to\R{n}$, $L_f(x_0)[x]=\mathbb{J}(x_0)\cdot x$
	\end{itemize}
\end{oss}

\noindent Through Definition~\ref{def:frechet_derivative}, the absolute and relative condition numbers of a matrix function can be defined as
\begin{equation}\label{eqn:cond_frechet}
	\operatorname{Cond}_{\text{abs}}(f,A)=\norm{L_f(A)},\qquad\operatorname{Cond}_{\text{rel}}(f,A)=\frac{\norm{L_f(A)}\norm{A}}{\norm{f(A)}}
\end{equation}
with the same notational convention~\ref{notaz:kappa_f}.

In Equation~\eqref{eqn:cond_frechet}, the norm of the Fréchet differential at $A$ appears. This norm is defined analogously to the norm of any linear operator (including matrices) as
\begin{equation}\label{eqn:def_norm_frechet}
	\norm{L_f(A)}\coloneqq\max_{\substack{E\in\C{n}{n}\\ E\ne 0}}\frac{\norm{L_f(A)[E]}}{\norm{E}}.
\end{equation} 
The norm of the operator $L_f(A)$ is the one induced by the norms of the input and output vector spaces. Being them $\C{n}{n}$, these norms are matrix norms.  

\begin{oss*}
	Note that from Remark~\ref{oss:frechet_generalizza} it follows that the~\eqref{eqn:cond_frechet} generalizes the definitions of condition numbers for differentiable vector-valued functions that we encountered in Section~\ref{sect:conditioning_general}.
\end{oss*}

The differential $\dd f(x)$ of a function $f\colon\R{n}\to\R{n}$ at a point $x\in\R{n}$ is a linear map from $\R{n}$ to $\R{n}$ which is represented in the canonical basis by the Jacobian matrix. An analogous phenomenon happens for matrix functions: the action of the Fréchet derivative can closely related to the multiplication by a matrix. Unlike the $\R{n}$ case however, the isomorphism is not the identity.

\begin{definizione}
	Let $\operatorname{Vec}\colon\C{n}{n}\to\C{n^2}$ be the function that ``stacks all the columns of a matrix'', i.e. let it be defined as 
	\[
		\begin{array}{rccl}
			\operatorname{Vec}\colon & \C{n}{n} & \longrightarrow & \C{n^2}\\
				& (X_{i,j})_{i,j\in[n]} & \longmapsto & \qty(\operatorname{Vec}X)_{(j-1)n+i}.
		\end{array}
	\]
\end{definizione}
\noindent Now consider the diagram 
\[
\xymatrix{
	\C{n^2} \ar[r]^{\psi_A} & \C{n^2} \\
	\C{n}{n} \ar[u]^{\operatorname{Vec}} \ar[r]_{L_f(A)} & \C{n}{n} \ar[u]^{\operatorname{Vec}}
}
\]
which commutes since $\operatorname{Vec}$ is an isomorphism. The linear function $\psi_A$ is represented by a square matrix $K=K(A)\in\C{n^2}{n^2}$. In other terms, for all directions $E\in\C{n}{n}$ it holds that
\begin{equation}\label{eqn:frechet_vec}
	\operatorname{Vec}\qty(L_f(A)[E])=K(A)\cdot\operatorname{Vec}(E).
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eqn:frechet_vec} not only is very useful for deriving properties of the Fréchet derivative, but can also be exploited by algorithms, as we will see in the following sections.

The matrix $K(A)$ is often referred to as the \emph{Kronecker representation} of $L_f(A)$ because, in many cases, it can be explicitly written by using the \emph{Kronecker product}, whose definition we now recall.
\begin{definizione}\label{def:kronecker_product}
	The \emph{Kronecker product} of $A\in\C{m}{n}$ and $B\in\C{p}{q}$ is the block matrix $A\otimes B\coloneqq(a_{i,j}B)\in\C{mp}{nq}$.
\end{definizione}
The Kronecker product satisfies many useful identities, such as
\begin{align}
	\qty(A\otimes B)^{H} &= A^H\otimes B^H\label{eqn:hermitian_kron}\\
	\operatorname{Vec}(AXB) &= \big(B^{\!\top}\otimes A\big)\cdot\operatorname{Vec}(X)\label{eqn:vec_kron_identity}
\end{align}


\begin{esempio}
	Let $f(z)=z^2$ and let $A\in\C{n}{n}$. Then $f(A)=A^2$, and -- by using the definition -- it holds that
	\begin{align*}
		f(A+E) - f(A) &= (A+E)^2 - A^2 = \qty(A^2 + AE + EA + E^2) - A^2\\
		 &= \underbrace{AE+EA}_{=L_f(A)[E]} + \underbrace{E^2}_{\mathclap{\ \in o(\norm{E})}}.
	\end{align*}
	By using the linearity of $\operatorname{Vec}$ and the identity~\eqref{eqn:vec_kron_identity} it can be easily proven that the matrix $I\otimes A + A^{\top}\otimes I$ satisfies Equation~\eqref{eqn:frechet_vec}, and must therefore equal the Kronecker representation $K(A)$ of $L_f(A)$.
	
	The same results can be obtained by using the properties of Section~\ref{subs:frechet_properties}.
\end{esempio}

If the vector spaces $\C{n}{n}$ and $\C{n^2}$ are equipped with, respectively, the inner products 
$\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_F$ and $\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_2$, where the latter is the 
standard Hermitian inner product $\langle u,v\rangle_2=u^{H}v$
and the former is defined as $\langle U,V\rangle_F\coloneqq\tr(U^{H}V)$, 
then $\operatorname{Vec}$ is an \emph{isometric isomorphism}, i.e. it satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{eqn:vec_isometry}
	\langle U,V\rangle_F = \langle\operatorname{Vec}(U),\operatorname{Vec}(V)\rangle_2\qquad\forall\,U,V\in\C{n}{n}.
\end{equation}
This can be easily proved from first principles.

By introducing an inner product on $\C{n}{n}$ we can talk about the \emph{adjoint} of the 
Fréchet derivative of $f$ at $A$, which we denote as $L_f(A)^{H}$. We recall that the
adjoint of a linear map satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{eqn:frechet_adjoint_def}
	\left\langle X,L_f(A)^{H}[Y]\right\rangle_F = \left\langle L_f(A)[X],Y\right\rangle_F.
\end{equation}
Let $M(A)\in\C{\cramped{n^2}}{\cramped{n^2}}$ be the Kronecker representation of $L_f(A)^{H}$.
Using $\operatorname{Vec}$, Equation~\eqref{eqn:frechet_adjoint_def} becomes
\[
	\left\langle\operatorname{Vec}(X),M(A)\operatorname{Vec}(Y)\right\rangle_2 = 
	\left\langle K(A)\operatorname{Vec}(X),\operatorname{Vec}(Y)\right\rangle_2,
\]
from which it follows that $M(A)=K(A)^{H}$. Using this fact, the properties of the Kronecker product and Corollary~\ref{cor:K(A)_power_series}, it can be proven that when $A\in\R{n}{n}$ and $f\colon\R{n}{n}\to\R{n}{n}$ has a power series, the adjoint is given by $L_f(A)^{H}=L_f(A^{\top})$. In the complex case, $L_f(A)^{H}=L_{\bar{f}}(A^{H})$ where $\bar{f}(z)\coloneqq\overline{f(\bar{z})}$. In particular,
$L_f(A)[E]=\qty(L_{\bar{f}}(A^{H})[E^{H}])^{\cramped{H}}$ for all $E\in\C{n}{n}$.

The adjoint of the Fréchet derivative can be used to estimate the condition number, as we will briefly discuss in Section~\ref{subs:compute_estimate_cond}. 

\subsection{Properties of the Fréchet derivative}\label{subs:frechet_properties}
The Fréchet derivative enjoys most of the usual properties of derivatives. The sum, product, inverse and chain rules are all satisfied. The rules can be stated, in order, as follows:
\begin{subequations}\label{eqn:frechet_rules}
\begin{align}
	L_{f+g}(A) & = E\mapsto L_f(A)[E] + L_g(A)[E]\nonumber \\
	L_{f\cdot g}(A) & = E\mapsto\big(L_f(A)[E]\cdot g(A) + f(A)\cdot L_g(A)[E]\big)\label{eqn:frechet_prod_rule}\\
	L_{f^{-1}}(f(A)) & = 
		\begin{multlined}[t]
			E\mapsto L_f(A)^{-1}[E],\\ 
			\text{or equivalently}\ L_f(A)\big[L_{f^{-1}}\qty(f(A))[E]\big]=E
		\end{multlined}\nonumber \\
	L_{f\circ g}(A) & = L_f(g(A))\circ L_g(A) = E\mapsto L_f\qty(g(A))\big[L_g\qty(A)\qty[E]\big].\label{eqn:frechet_chain_rule}
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
\noindent We recall four of the many computation-oriented properties of the Fréchet derivative.

By analogy with the scalar case $\dd f(x)=f'(x)\dd{x}$, one may be tempted to define the differential of a matrix function at $A$ evaluated along $E$ as $f'(A)E$. This is \emph{not} the same as $L_f(A)[E]$ in general, but if $A$ and $E$ commute then $ L_f(A)[E]=f'(A)E$.

If $f\colon\Omega\subseteq\C{n}\to\C{n}$ is $2n-1$ times continuously differentiable on $\Omega$, then 
\begin{equation}\label{eqn:mathias_trick}
	f\left(\begin{array}{c|c}
		A & E \\ \hline & A
	\end{array}\right)=
	\left(\begin{array}{c|c}
		f(A) & L_f(A)[E] \\ \hline & f(A)
	\end{array}\right)
\end{equation}
holds for any $A\in\C{n}{n}$ such that $\Lambda(A)\subseteq\Omega$ and for any direction $E\in\C{n}{n}$ for which $L_f(A)$ is defined. The reader is referred to~\cite[Thm.~3.6]{higham:matfun_book} for a proof of this result and additional details.

Thanks to the formula in~\eqref{eqn:mathias_trick}, the problem of evaluating the Fréchet derivative is converted to that of computing the function on a matrix of twice the size. This computation returns both $f(A)$ and $L_f(A)[E]$, similarly to what happens in forward mode automatic differentiation.
However, the cost of evaluating a function on a matrix of size $n$ is usually $O(n^3)$, so the cost of computing $f(A)$ and $L_f(A)[E]$ using~\eqref{eqn:mathias_trick} is $8$ times that of computing $f(A)$ alone.

Using~\eqref{eqn:mathias_trick} one can prove the following Lemma.
\begin{lemma}(\cite[Cor.~3.12]{higham:matfun_book})
	Let $f\colon\Sigma\subseteq\C\to\C$ be $2n-1$ times continuously differentiable on $\Sigma$ and let $A\in\C{n}{n}$ have spectrum in $\Sigma$ and be diagonalizable: $A=VDV^{-1}$, $D=\operatorname{Diag}(\lambda_i)$. Then 
	\[
		L_f(A)[E] = V\cdot L_f(D)[V^{-1}EV]\cdot V^{-1}.
	\]
\end{lemma}

Lemma~\ref{lemma:frechet_power_series} (see~\cite[Problem~3.6]{higham:matfun_book}) gives an explicit formula for the Fréchet differential of matrix functions that have a power series representation.
\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:frechet_power_series}
	Let $f$ be defined by the power series $f(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}c_k(z-\alpha)^k$ with radius of convergence $r>0$. If $A\in\C{n}{n}$ is such that $\norm{A-\alpha I}<r$ then the Fréchet derivative of $f$ at $A$ is given by
	\begin{equation}\label{eqn:frechet_power_series}
		L_f(A) = E\mapsto\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}c_k\sum_{t=1}^k(A-\alpha I)^{t-1}E(A-\alpha I)^{k-t}
	\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
	The proof will only be sketched.
	As a consequence of the product rule~\eqref{eqn:frechet_prod_rule}, for $k>1$,
	\begin{equation}\label{eqn:differential_kth_power}
	\begin{aligned}
		L_{z\mapsto z^k}(A) & = E\mapsto\qty(L_{z\mapsto z^{k-1}}(A)[E]\cdot A+A^{k-1}\cdot L_{z\mapsto z}(A)[E])\\
		 & = E\mapsto\qty(L_{z\mapsto z^{k-1}}(A)[E]\cdot A+A^{k-1}\cdot E)
	\end{aligned}
	\end{equation}
	where $z\mapsto z^{k}$ denotes the function which raises its argument to the $k$th power.
	Using~\eqref{eqn:differential_kth_power} one can prove by induction on $k\ge 1$ that 
	\[
		L_{z\mapsto z^k}\qty(A-\alpha I) = E\mapsto\sum_{t=1}^k\qty(A-\alpha I)^{t-1}E\qty(A-\alpha I)^{k-t}.
	\]	
	A classical result in complex analysis states that power series can be differentiated term by term inside their disk of convergence. Thus,
	\[
		L_f(A) = L_{z\mapsto\sum_{\cramped{k=0}}^{\infty} c_kz^k}(A) =  \sum_{k=1}^{\infty}c_k L_{z\mapsto z^k}(A)
	\]
	for all $A\in\C{n}{n}$ s.t. $\norm{A-\alpha I}<r$, which completes the proof.
\end{proof}
\begin{corollario_lemma}\label{cor:K(A)_power_series}
	Let $f$ and $A$ be as in Lemma~\ref{lemma:frechet_power_series}. The matrix $K(A)\in\C{\cramped{n^2}}{\cramped{n^2}}$ that represents $L_f(A)$ is given by
	\begin{equation}\label{eqn:K(A)_power_series}
		\C{\cramped{n^2}}{\cramped{n^2}}\ni K(A)=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}c_k\sum_{t=1}^k\big(A^{\top}-\alpha I\big)^{{t-1}}\otimes\big(A-\alpha I\big)^{k-t}
	\end{equation}
\end{corollario_lemma}
\begin{proof}
	The matrix $K(A)$ must satisfy Equation~\eqref{eqn:frechet_vec}. By applying the $\operatorname{Vec}$ operator to $L_f(A)[E]$ where $L_f(A)$ is as in~\eqref{eqn:frechet_power_series}, exploiting the linearity of $\operatorname{Vec}$ and by making use of the identity~\eqref{eqn:vec_kron_identity}, the result in Equation~\eqref{eqn:K(A)_power_series} is readily obtained.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Computing or estimating the condition number}\label{subs:compute_estimate_cond}
The problem of computing or estimating $\kappa_f(A)$ essentially reduces that of computing or estimating the norm of the Fréchet differential at $A$. There are two aspects to this last problem, namely the computation of $L_f(A)$ and the computation, or estimation, of its norm. 

Recall that the condition number is defined with respect to a norm. A norm on $\C{n}{n}$ has to be chosen, then the norm of $L_f(A)$ will be its subordinate operator norm, as introduced by Equation~\eqref{eqn:def_norm_frechet}. However, as Equation~\eqref{eqn:def_norm_frechet} -- which introduced its definition -- also suggests, $\norm{L_f(A)}$ doesn't have a simple expression, in general. 

Lemma~\ref{lemma:fro_norm_frechet}, an important consequence of Equation~\eqref{eqn:vec_isometry}, states that one exception is the Frobenius norm. 
\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:fro_norm_frechet}
	Let $K(A)\in\C{\cramped{n^2}}{\cramped{n^2}}$ be as in Equation~\eqref{eqn:frechet_vec}. 
	Then $\norm{L_f(A)}_F=\norm{K(A)}_2$
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
	As a consequence of~\eqref{eqn:vec_isometry}, $\norm{X}_F=\norm{\operatorname{Vec}(X)}_2$
	for all $X\in\C{n}{n}$. 
	\begin{align*}
		\norm{L_f(A)}_F &= \max_{\substack{E\in\C{n}{n}\\ E\ne 0}}\frac{\norm{L_f(A)[E]}_F}{\norm{E}_F}=
		\max_{\substack{E\in\C{n}{n}\\ E\ne 0}}\frac{\norm{\operatorname{Vec}\big(L_f(A)[E]\big)}_2}{\norm{\operatorname{Vec}(E)}_2}=\\
		& \max_{\substack{E\in\C{n}{n}\\ E\ne 0}}\frac{\norm{K(A)\operatorname{Vec}(E)}_2}{\norm{\operatorname{Vec}(E)}_2}=
		\max_{\substack{x\in\C{\cramped{n^2}}\\ x\ne 0}}\frac{\norm{K(A)x}_2}{\norm{x}_2}=\norm{K(A)}_2.\qedhere
	\end{align*}
\end{proof}
Lemma~\ref{lemma:fro_norm_frechet} suggests that $\norm{L_f(A)}_F$ can be exactly computed as shown in Algorithm~\ref{alg:exact_cond_n}; by forming the $n^2\times n^2$ matrix $K(A)$, then $\kappa_f(A)=\norm{K(A)}_2\norm{A}_F/\norm{f(A)}_F$. 
\begin{algorithm}[H]
	\caption{\emph{Exact $\kappa_f(A)$ computation in the Frobenius norm~\cite[Alg.~3.17]{higham:matfun_book}}}
	\label{alg:exact_cond_n}
	\begin{algorithmic}[1]
		\For{$j=1,\dots,n$}
			\For{$i=1,\dots,n$}
				\State $Y \gets L_f(A)\big[e_{i}e_{j}^{\cramped{\top}}\big]$
				\State $\SubMat{K}{}{(j-1)n+i} = \operatorname{Vec}(A)$
			\EndFor
		\EndFor
		\State $\kappa_f(A) = \norm{K(A)}_2\norm{A}_F / \norm{f(A)}_F$
	\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}
Algorithm~\ref{alg:exact_cond_n} explicitly forms $K(A)$; this assumes that it's possible to compute the Fréchet derivative along every coordinate direction. An ``oracle'' implementing the action $E\to L_f(A)[E]$ provides a way to do so.
Section~\ref{subs:expm_sas_miscellanea} describes how this ``oracle'' is obtained for the matrix exponential.

Algorithms for computing $f(A)$ usually cost $O(n^3)$ flops; assuming that evaluating $L_f(A)$ costs $O(n^3)$ flops, Algorithm~\ref{alg:exact_cond_n} has the prohibitive cost of $O(n^5)$ flops. In addition, the memory cost of storing $K(A)$ should be consiered, as well as the cost of computing $\norm{K(A)}_2$. This last cost depends on the method that is used to compute the 2-norm of the $n^2$-by-$n^2$ matrix $K(A)$. For example, the SVD would yield a $O(n^6)$ cost; and the power method would yield a cost between $O(n^4)$ and $O(n^6)$, depending on its convergence rate.

In Algorithm~\ref{alg:exact_cond_n}, $K(A)$ is explicitly formed and its 2-norm is computed. %Notice that the algorithm constructs the matrix by repeatedly applying the action of the Fréchet derivative, which is closely related to that of $K(A)$ via Equation~\eqref{eqn:frechet_vec}. 
As just mentioned, an approximation of $\kappa_f(A)$ in the Frobenius norm could be obtaining by applying the power method on $K(A)^{\cramped{H}}K(A)$. In fact, $\norm{K(A)}_2=\rho\big(K(A)^{\cramped{H}}K(A)\big)^{1/2}$, so once the dominant eigenvalue of $K(A)^{\cramped{H}}K(A)$ is computed, the desired norm is readily obtained.

The power method does not explicitly need the matrix, it just needs a way to apply it to a vector. In our case, similarly to Algorithm~\ref{alg:exact_cond_n}, this can be achieved by an ``oracle'' for $E\mapsto L_f(A)[E]$ and one for $E\mapsto L_f(A)^{\cramped{H}}[E]$. The resulting algorithm is available at~\cite[Alg.~3.20]{higham:matfun_book}. The power method is less computationally demanding than Algorithm~\ref{alg:exact_cond_n}, and doesn't need to explicitly form and store the matrix. However, it returns just an approximation of the desired quantity, and requires two different ``oracles'' instead of one.
\smallskip 

Let us now consider the computation of the condition number in the $1$-norm. Again, the real issue is the computation of $\norm{L_f(A)}_1$. Unfortunately, there isn't a result like Lemma~\ref{lemma:fro_norm_frechet} stating that the $1$-norm of $L_f(A)$ is exactly equal to some norm of its associated matrix $K(A)$. However, the $1$-norms of $K(A)$ and of $L_f(A)$ are related via the following result.
\begin{lemma}
	Let $A\in\C{n}{n}$ and $f$ be any function defined on the spectrum of $A$. It holds that
	\[
		\frac{\norm{L_f(A)}_1}{n}\le\norm{K(A)}_1\le n\norm{L_f(A)}_1.
	\]
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
	See the proof of~\cite[Lemma~3.18]{higham:matfun_book}.
\end{proof}
So, by using $\norm{K(A)}_1$ in Algorithm~\ref{alg:exact_cond_n} and by replacing the norms at line 7, we obtain an algorithm that produces an estimate that agrees with $\kappa_f(A)$ to within a factor $n$. Since error bounds invariably contain pessimistic constants that are polynomial in $n$, this discrepancy is usually not problematic. 

The computation of $\norm{K(A)}_1$ is generally inexpensive compared to that of $\norm{K(A)}_2$; nevertheless, it requires the full matrix $K(A)$, which -- as already discussed -- we would prefer not to form explicitly.

Indeed, there exist $1$-norm analogues of the power method that estimate the norm of a matrix using only the action of the matrix and of its adjoint. 
One such method is presented in~\cite[Alg.~3.21]{higham:matfun_book}; for a thorough discussion, see also~\cite[§~15.3]{higham:accuracy_book} and the referenes therein. This algorithm forms the basis of the condition number estimation routines in \textsc{lapack}, such as \texttt{xLACN2}~\cite{lapack:book}, and is employed by higher-level programming environments as well. In \matlab{}, it is used by the \texttt{rcond} function, which estimates the reciprocal condition number. Julia does not natively provide a function for estimating the matrix 1-norm. However, the \texttt{MatrixEquations.jl} library~\cite{sw:MatrixEquations} provides an interface to \textsc{lapack}'s \texttt{xLACN2} through the function \texttt{opnorm1est}.

\begin{oss*}
	Since \texttt{opnorm1est} is a direct call to \textsc{lapack}, it can only accept data in double or single precision.
\end{oss*}

The algoritm in~\cite[Alg.~3.21]{higham:matfun_book} is a very reliable estimator of the matrix 1-norm. In fact, it typically requires 4 or 5 iterations to compute its estimate, which is frequently an exact one. Although it can produce an arbitrarily poor approximation on specifically constructed counterexamples, it almost invariably produces an estimate correct to within a factor $3$, independently from the size $n$ of the matrix.

In \matlab{}, 1-norm estimates can be computed using the \texttt{normest1} function, which implements the block generalization of~\cite[Alg.~3.21]{higham:matfun_book} introduced in~\cite{higham:hiti_normest1}. The algorithm repeatedly computes products of the form $MV$, where $M\in\C{n}{n}$ is the matrix whose 1-norm is to be estimated and $V\in\C{n}{q}$, with $q$ being a small positive integer. In the \matlab{} implementation, the default value is $q=2$. Setting $q=1$ recovers Algorithm~\cite[Alg.~3.21]{higham:matfun_book}. 

The function \texttt{normest1} is written entirely in MATLAB and can also accept an ``oracle'' implementing the action of the linear operator whose 1-norm is to be estimated. In \matlab{}, such an oracle is represented by a function handle. In Julia, the corresponding abstraction is a \texttt{LinearMap}, accepted by \texttt{MatrixEquations.jl}'s \texttt{opnorm1est}.

\section{Computational techniques for matrix functions}
The exposition of computational methods for generic matrix functions will be instrumental 
to the main focus of this essay, i.e. the computation of the matrix exponential using scaling and squaring.
Therefore the two principal families of general-purpose methods will be presented only at a high level,
while methods to compute matrix polynomials will be described in greater detail.

\subsection{The Schur-Parlett method}
In theory, $f(A)$ could be computed by directly applying Definition~\ref{def:matfun_Jordan}; i.e. by computing the Jordan canonical form $A=VJV^{-1}$, so that $f(A)=Vf(J)V^{-1}$ where $f(J)$ is given in~\eqref{eqn:f_Jordan_block}. This strategy is not viable in practice, because the Jordan canonical form cannot be reliably computed in floating point arithmetic.
\begin{oss*}
	Even if it could, the computed result may be a poor approximation of $f(A)$ anyway. This is because the accuracy of the Jordan form method is limited by the conditioning of the matrix $V$. This phenomenon is discussed in Section~\ref{subs:diagonalization} for the diagonalizable case, but the analysis holds in this more general case as well.
\end{oss*}
One of the most basic tenets of numerical linear algebra is that ``\emph{anything that the Jordan decomposition can do, the Schur decomposition can do better!}''~\cite{vanloan:StudyMatrixExponential75}
\smallskip 

The Schur-Parlett method exploits the fact that the elements of a function of a upper triangular matrix can be computed through a clever recurrence.

In its simplest form, the method follows these key steps: a Schur form of $A=UTU^{H}$ is computed, so that $F(A)=Uf(T)U^{H}$. The diagonal elements of $F\coloneqq f(T)$ can be computed trivially. The off-diagonal elements can be recovered by reading the identity $TF=FT$ -- which holds by Lemma~\ref{lemma:matfun_trivial_properties} -- in the $(i,j)$th entry ($i<j$) and solving for the desired quantity. Indeed, 
\begin{equation}\label{eqn:parlett_recurrence}
	T_{i,i}F_{i,j}-F_{i,j}T_{j,j} = \sum_{k=i}^{j-1}F_{i,k}T_{k,j} - \sum_{k=i+1}^jT_{i,k}F_{k,j},
\end{equation}
thus $F_{i,j}$ depends only on $\SubMat{F}{i+1,j}{j}$ and on $\SubMat{F}{i}{i,j-1}$.  

\begin{oss*}
	Note that the recurrence in Equation~\eqref{eqn:parlett_recurrence} breaks down if $T_{i,i}=T_{j,j}$. A way around this difficulty is to employ a block form of the recurrence, which involves solving Sylvester equations instead of performing scalar divisions. This topic is beyond the aims of this essay, the reader is referred to~\cite[§~4.6]{higham:matfun_book} for further details. 
\end{oss*}

\begin{oss*}
	When $A$ is a $2$-by-$2$ upper triangular matrix with distinct eigenvalues, the computation of $f(A)$ using Schur-Parlett can be formulated precisely as in Equation~\eqref{eqn:matfun_2by2_tri}.	
\end{oss*}

Schur-Parlett can be employed if no specific algorithm for $f$ is applicable. This is discussed in~\cite[§~9]{higham:matfun_book}. Fortunately, this is not the case for the matrix exponential.

\subsection{Matrix iterations}
Matrix iteration methods produce a sequence of matrices $X_1,\dots,X_k$ so that $X_k\xlongrightarrow{k\to\infty}f(A)$. The iterates take the form $X_{k+1}=g(X_k)$, where $g$ may depend on $A$; and $X_0$ is almost never arbitrary, rather it's a fixed function of $A$ such as $X_0=I$ or $X_0=A$.

A standard means of deriving matrix iterations is to apply Newton’s method to an algebraic equation satisfied by $f(A)$ and then to choose $X_0$ so that the iteration formula is simplified. 
One such example is the Denman-Beavers iteration for the matrix square root, which is described in~\cite[§~6.3]{higham:matfun_book}. 

Matrix iterations are not in the scope of this thesis, more details on the topic can be found in~\cite[§~4.9]{higham:matfun_book}.


\subsection{Matrix polynomials}
Unlike the scalar case, evaluating a polynomial $p(x)=\sum_{k=0}^m a_kx^k$ of degree $m\ge 1$ at a matrix argument by explicitly forming the matrix powers has the same asymptotical cost as evaluating it via the Horner's method (see~\cite[Algs.~4.2,4.3]{higham:matfun_book}). This is because both methods perform $m-1$ matrix multiplications. Since each one generally requires $O(n^3)$ flops, compared to the $O(n^2)$ typical cost of a linear combination, matrix multiplications dominate the computational cost.

\subsubsection{The Paterson-Stockmeyer method}\label{subs:paterson-stockmeyer}
A matrix polynomial can be evaluated with $O(\sqrt{m})$ matrix-matrix multiplications, using the Paterson-Stockmeyer method~\cite{paterson_stockmeyer:article}.
In the Paterson-Stockmeyer scheme an integer index $\nu\in[m]$ (which we will call the \emph{``batch size''} throughout this essay) is chosen, $p(A)$ is rewritten as a polynomial in $A^{\nu}$ of degree $\le m$, whose coefficients are linear combinations of $I,A,\dots,A^{\nu-1}$. We now describe the method more rigorously.

The polynomial $p(A)$ is written as 
\begin{equation}\label{eqn:ps_poly}
	p(A) = \sum_{k=0}^{r}B_k(A)\cdot\qty(A^{\nu})^k\qq{with}r=\left\lfloor\frac{m}{\nu}\right\rfloor
\end{equation}
where the coefficients $B_k(A)$ are given by
\begin{align*}
	B_k(A)&\coloneqq\begin{dcases}
		\sum_{j=0}^{\nu-1}a_{\nu k+j}A^j & k=0,\dots,r-1\\
		\sum_{j=0}^{[m]_{\nu}}a_{\nu k+j}A^j & k=r
	\end{dcases}\\
	&\phantom{:}=\begin{dcases}
		a_{\nu k+\nu-1}A^{\nu-1}+\dots+a_{\nu k+1}A+a_{\nu k}I & k=0,\dots,r-1\\
		a_mA^{m-\nu r}+\dots+a_{\nu r+1}A+a_{\nu r}I & k=r,
	\end{dcases}
\end{align*}
where $[m]_{\nu}$ is the remainder of the integer division of $m$ by $\nu$, i.e. $m = \nu r + [m]_{\nu}$.
\begin{esempio}
	For $m=6,\nu=3$ we have 
	\begin{align*}
	p(A)&=a_6\qty(A^3)^{2} + \qty(a_5A^2+a_4A+a_3)A^3+\qty(a_2A^2+a_1A+a_0).
	\intertext{For $m=6,\nu=2$ we have }
	p(A)&=a_6\qty(A^2)^3+\qty(a_5A+a_4)(A^2)^2+\qty(a_3A+a_2)A^2+\qty(a_1A+a_0).
	\intertext{For $m=8,\nu=3$ we have}
	p(A)&=\begin{multlined}[t]\qty(a_8A^2+a_7A+a_6)\qty(A^3)^2+\\
		+\qty(a_5A^2+a_4A+a_3)A^3+\qty(a_2A^2+a_1A+a_0).\end{multlined}
	\end{align*}
\end{esempio}
The algorithm proceeds as follows. The powers $I,A,A^2,\dots, A^{\nu}$ are computed beforehand. This requires $\nu-1$ matrix multiplications. Then~\eqref{eqn:ps_poly} is evaluated through the Horner scheme, forming the coefficients $B_k(A)$ as needed. Note that the cost of forming the $B_k(A)$ coefficients is negligible, because the powers of $A$ have already been computed.

The total number of matrix multiplications is therefore
\begin{equation}\label{eqn:ps_cost}
	C_m(\nu)=
	\begin{multlined}[t]
		\overbrace{\nu - 1}^{\mathclap{\text{Powers of $A$}}} + \\
		\underbrace{\left\lfloor\frac{m}{\nu}\right\rfloor - \mathbbm{1}_{\qty{[m]_{\nu}=0}}}_{\mathclap{\text{Evaluation of~\eqref{eqn:ps_poly}}}}
	\end{multlined}
	\qquad \mathbbm{1}_{\qty{[m]_{\nu}=0}}\coloneqq\begin{dcases*}
		1 & if $\nu$ divides $m$\\
		0 & otherwise.
	\end{dcases*}
\end{equation}
By taking its derivative with respect to $\nu$ (and dropping the $\lfloor\phantom{r}\rfloor$ in $r$), we see that $C_m(\nu)$ in~\eqref{eqn:ps_cost} is minimized by $\sqrt{m}$; thus the cost is approximately minimized by either $\nu=\lfloor\sqrt{m}\rfloor$ or $\nu=\lceil\sqrt{m}\rceil$. With either choice of ``batch size'', evaluating a polynomial of degree $m$ at a $n$-by-$n$ matrix costs $O(\sqrt{m}n^3)$ flops. See also~\cite[§~1]{fasi:ps_optimality}.

It can be easily seen that the two extreme cases $\nu=1$ and $\nu=m$ correspond to the Horner and to the explicit powers evaluation schemes, respectively.

\begin{oss*}
	The Paterson-Stockmeyer method comes with its tradeoffs. The lower number of matrix-matrix multiplications comes with additional matrix-scalar products and matrix sums. As already discussed, these operations are cheaper than matrix-matrix multiplications.
	
	As many intermediate quantities such as the matrix powers $I,A,\dots,A^{\nu}$ have to be repeatedly used, the Paterson-Stockmeyer has a higher memory footprint than the other two methods: it requires more units of storage and more memory operations. This memory cost can be reduced by computing $p(A)$ one column at a time, at the price of additional matrix-matrix multiplications. For details, see~\cite[§~4.2]{higham:matfun_book} and the references therein.
\end{oss*}

As we will see in Section~\ref{sect:exp_mp_description}, the Paterson-Stockmeyer is an essential building block of the multiprecision algorithm for the matrix exponential that this essay presents. 

The same error bound holds for the three methods to evaluate matrix polynomials considered so far, see~\cite[Thm.~4.5]{higham:matfun_book} for the precise statement. The essential point is that if there is significant cancellation in forming the polynomial $p$, then the error in the computed result can be large. 

\subsubsection{Beyond Paterson-Stockmeyer}\label{subs:beyond_p-s}
The Paterson-Stockmeyer method is not the fastest known algorithm for evaluating polynomials of matrices: in the same article that introduced their method~\cite{paterson_stockmeyer:article}, Paterson and Stockmeyer discuss a technique that requires fewer matrix-matrix multiplications; which still underlies all methods that try to improve on the method that carries their name. 

Research on faster algorithms for evaluating matrix polynomials is still very active at the time of writing. Although the topic is interesting, it is not central to this thesis. Therefore, we limit ourselves to a simple introduction of the general idea. The reader is referred to the cited literature for further details.
\smallskip

Let us fix a number $C\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\{0\}$ of matrix-matrix multiplications, and consider the recursive scheme 
\begin{subequations}\label{eqn:degopt_poly:scheme}
\begin{gather}
	\begin{dcases}
		\mu_{-1}=I\\ \mu_0=A
	\end{dcases}, \qquad
	\mu_r= \qty(\sum_{i=-1}^{r-1}m_{a,r,i}\mu_i)\qty(\sum_{i=-1}^{r-1}m_{b,r,i}\mu_i)\label{eqn:degopt_poly:intermediates}\\
\intertext{so that} 
	Y=\sum_{i=1}^C y_i\mu_i. \label{eqn:degopt_poly:final}
\end{gather}	
\end{subequations}
$Y$ as in~\eqref{eqn:degopt_poly:final} is a polynomial in $A$ of degree at most $2^{C}$ that can be evaluated with $C$ matrix-matrix multiplications. See also~\cite[§~2]{paterson_stockmeyer:article} and~\cite[Eqn.~(2)]{fasi:jfr23_compgraphs}.

\begin{oss*}
	The procedure described in~\eqref{eqn:degopt_poly:scheme} proceeds in $C$ steps. Each step forms the product of two linear combinations of the quantities produced at the previous steps. Then, a final linear combination is performed.
\end{oss*}

It can be proven that in~\eqref{eqn:degopt_poly:scheme} the number of parameters $m_{a,\_,\_}$, $m_{b,\_,\_}$, $y_{\_}$ that appear is $O(C^2)$. It follows that not all polynomials of degree at most $2^C$ can be evaluated using that scheme, especially if $C$ is large. In fact, to evaluate a matrix polynomial $p(A)=\sum_{i=0}^{2^C}a_iA^i$ using~\eqref{eqn:degopt_poly:scheme}, the parameters must be obtained from the coefficients of the polynomial. In general, this yields a system of up to $2^C$ nonlinear equations in $O(C^2)$ unknowns. 

In some cases, the system can be solved and may even admit more than one solution; see~\cite[§~2]{sastre:siad25_beyond}. A solution can be obtained using different techniques, such as symbolic arithmetic~\cite{sastre:si21_efficient, sastre:boosting_19:expm}. Another possibility is to formulate the general scheme in~\eqref{eqn:degopt_poly:scheme} is formulated as a computational graph, so that the choice of the parameters is interpreted as an optimization problem. See~\cite{fasi:jfr23_compgraphs} for further details.
\bigskip

\noindent We conclude this section on the evaluation of matrix polynomials by mentioning the \emph{binary powering algorithm}, because the scaling and squaring algorithm uses a special case of it. The binary powering algorithm efficiently computes $A^m$ (i.e. it evaluates the monomial $x^m$ at $A$) by using the binary representation of $m$ instead of explicitly forming all of the previous powers of the matrix. More details can be found in~\cite[§~4.1]{higham:matfun_book}.
