\chapter{Matrix Functions}\label{capitolo:2}
This chapter introduces the matrix functions, their definitions, their basic properties and their conditioning. This chapter is aimed at laying the ground for the presentation that will be given in Chapter~\ref{capitolo:3} of the Matrix Exponential, the matrix function this essay focuses on. Therefore the treatment will be concise, giving multiple specific references for further details.

\section{Introduction}
The general idea behind the functions of a matrix is that a function $f\colon\Sigma\subseteq\C\to\C$ is extended to its counterpart on square matrices, $f\colon\Omega\subseteq\C{n}{n}\to\C{n}{n}$, in such a way that the core properties are preserved.
This is \emph{very} different from applying (the scalar) $f$ to the elements of $A\in\C{n}{n}$.

\begin{esempio*}
	By analogy with the complex exponential, the matrix exponential function $\exp\colon\C{n}{n}\to\C{n}{n}$ should map the zero matrix to the identity matrix.  
	Instead, the element-wise application of the complex exponential, denoted -- using Julia notation -- by $e.$, returns $e.(0_{n\times n})=\mathrm{e}\,\mathrm{e}^{\!\top}$, where $\mathrm{e}\in\C{n}$ is the vector of all ones.
\end{esempio*}

\textcolor{red}{Funzioni di matrici che conoscono tutti: i polinomi di matrici, e la funzione $f(A)=A^{-1}$.}

We anticipate that, in order to be \emph{defined} on square matrices, $f$ must satisfy some additional properties that may not be necessary for the definition of the original scalar function $f$. Specifically, the existence of derivatives at certain points must be guaranteed, up to a certain order.

\section{Definitions of matrix function}
Matrix functions can be defined in three equivalent ways. 

\subsection*{Jordan form definition}
The first definition we introduce makes use of the Jordan form of $A$.

\begin{notazione}
	Let $\mu_g(\lambda)$ and $\mu_a(\lambda)$ denote, respectively, the geometric and arithmetic multiplicity of an eigenvalue $\lambda$.
	Let $\mathbb{C}\supset\hat{\Lambda}(A)=\qty{\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_s}$ be the \emph{spectrum} of $A$ with the convention that the eigenvalues are repeated with \emph{geometric multiplicity}. 
\end{notazione}
Then $A$ can be written in \emph{Jordan canonical form} as
\begin{equation}\label{eqn:jordan_A}
	A = V\,\operatorname{Blkdiag}\qty(J_{n_1}(\lambda_1),\dots,J_{n_s}(\lambda_s))\,V^{-1}
\end{equation}
where each $J_{n_k}(\lambda_k)$ is a \emph{Jordan block}, of the form 
\[
	J(\lambda_k)\coloneqq
	\begin{pNiceMatrix}[nullify-dots]
		\lambda_k & 1      &        & \\
		& \Ddots & \Ddots & \\
		&        &        & 1\\
		&        &        & \lambda_k\\                    
	\end{pNiceMatrix}\in\C{n_k}{n_k}.
\]
We recall that each of the $\mu_g(\lambda_k)$ ``copies'' of $\lambda_k$ is tied to an invariant subspace of size $n_k$. This invariant subspace is generated by a \emph{Jordan chain}, i.e. a set of \emph{generalized eigenvectors} $\qty{v_1,\dots,v_{n_k}}$ such that $v_j=(A-\lambda_kI)v_{j+1}\ (j\in[n_k-1])$ and $v_{n_k}\in\ker(A-\lambda_kI)^{n_k}\setminus\ker(A-\lambda_kI)^{n_k-1}$. 

We recall that the \emph{generalized eigenspace} of $\lambda_k$ is given by the direct sum of the aforementioned invariant subspaces, or equivalently, by 
\[
	\qty{v\in\mathbb{C}^n\mid \exists\,r\in\mathbb{N}\quad(A-\lambda_k I)^rv=0}\equiv\ker(A-\lambda_k I)^{n(\lambda_k)}
\]
where $n(\lambda_k)$ is the \emph{index} of $\lambda_k$, i.e. the size of the largest Jordan block in which $\lambda_k$ appears, or equivalently the multiplicity of $\lambda_k$ as a root of the minimal polynomial of $A$.
\begin{oss}
	Recall that the Jordan canonical form of $A$ is unique up to reordering of the blocks $J_{n_k}$; in particular, the block dimensions $n_1,\dots,n_k$ are unique. However, the change of basis $V$ is not unique. 
\end{oss}
More details on the Jordan canonical form can be found in~\cite[§~3]{horn_johnson_2012:book}. 

\noindent In order to be able to define $f$ on $A$ as in~\eqref{eqn:jordan_A}, the condition in the Definition~\ref{def:f_def_spectrum} needs to hold.
\begin{definizione}\label{def:f_def_spectrum}
	The function $f\colon\Sigma\subseteq\C\to\C$ is said to be \emph{defined on the spectrum of} $A$ if it is differentiable at least $n_k-1$ times on $\lambda_k$ for each $\lambda_k\in\hat{\Lambda}(A)$.
	
	Equivalently, $f$ is defined on the spectrum of $A$ if the values $f^{(j)}(\lambda)$ exist for all $j=0,\dots,n(\lambda)-1$, for all eigenvalues $\lambda\in\Lambda(A)$.
\end{definizione}

We can finally give the definition of matrix function via Jordan form.
\begin{definizione}\label{def:matfun_Jordan}
	Let $A\in\C{n}{n}$ have the Jordan canonical form~\eqref{eqn:jordan_A}, let $f$ be defined on the spectrum of $A$.
	Then $f$ is \emph{defined} on $A$, and
	\begin{equation*}
		f(A)\coloneqq V\,\operatorname{Blkdiag}\big(f(J_{n_1}(\lambda_1)),\dots,f(J_{n_k}(\lambda_k))\big)\, V^{-1}
	\end{equation*}
	where 
	\begin{equation}\label{eqn:f_Jordan_block}
		f\qty(J_{n_k}(\lambda_k))\coloneqq
		\begin{pNiceMatrix}[nullify-dots]
			f(\lambda_k) & f'(\lambda_k) & \Ldots & & \frac{f^{(n_k-1)}(\lambda_k)}{(n_k-1)!} \\
					     & f(\lambda_k)  & \Ddots & & \Vdots \\
						 & 		  		 & \Ddots & & \\
						 &        		 &        & & f'(\lambda_k)\\
						 &        		 &        & & f(\lambda_k) \\                    
		\end{pNiceMatrix}\in\C{n_k}{n_k}.
	\end{equation}
\end{definizione}

Definition~\ref{def:matfun_Jordan} comes natural if $f$ is a polynomial, in fact~\eqref{eqn:f_Jordan_block} can be proven by a simple computation.

\textcolor{red}{Dire: anche se a prima vista non sembra, è una buona definizione.}

\subsection*{Polynomial interpolation definition}
The second definition of matrix function is based on polynomial interpolation.

\begin{definizione}\label{def:matfun_poly}
	Let $f$ be defined on the spectrum of $A\in\C{n}{n}$, let $p\in\C\qty[t]$, with $\deg{p}<\sum_{\lambda_k\in\hat{\Lambda}(A)}n_k=n$ be a polynomial that interpolates $f$ and its derivatives on $\hat{\Lambda}(A)$, i.e. such that 
	\begin{equation}\label{eqn:interp_conditions}
		f^{(j)}(\lambda_k)=p^{(j)}(\lambda_k)\qquad\forall\,j=0,\dots,n_k-1\quad\forall\,\lambda_k\in\hat{\Lambda}(A).
	\end{equation}
	Then $f$ is \emph{defined} on $A$, and $f(A)\coloneqq p(A)$.
\end{definizione}
It can be proven that such a polynomial can always be constructed.

Some of the conditions in~\eqref{eqn:interp_conditions} are repeated if in $\hat{\Lambda}(A)$ there are repeated eigenvalues. However, the interpolation conditions relative to each ``copy'' of a repeated eigenvalue are consistent. Thus, they may be satisfied by a polynomial of degree lower than $n-1$. 

Notice that a polynomial $p\in\mathbb{C}[t]$ satisfies~\eqref{eqn:interp_conditions} if and only if, for each eigenvalue $\lambda\in\Lambda(A)$, $p$ interpolates $f$ on $\lambda$ up to the $\qty(n(\lambda)-1)$\emph{th} derivative. This requirement can thus replace~\eqref{eqn:interp_conditions} in Definition~\ref{def:matfun_poly}, together with $\deg{p}<\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda(A)}n(\lambda)$. There is only one polynomial, known as the \emph{Hermite polynomial}, that satisfies these conditions. There are explicit formulas for this polynomial, see also~\cite[§~1.2.2]{higham:matfun_book}.

It can easily be proven that, for $p,q\in\C\qty[t]$, $p(A)=q(A)$ if and only if $p$ and $q$ coincide on $\Lambda(A)$ (see~\cite[Thm.~1.3]{higham:matfun_book}). Therefore if $p$ and $q$ are polynomials that satisfy~\eqref{eqn:interp_conditions} then $f(A)=p(A)=q(A)$. So a polynomial of higher degree than necessary can nevertheless produce $f(A)$. A higher degree than necessary may be the result of imposing more interpolation conditions.

As a result, Definition~\ref{def:matfun_poly} is well posed, as it doesn't depend on the choice of the polynomial $p$ satisfying the interpolation conditions. 

\begin{oss*}
	In Definition~\ref{def:matfun_poly} no change of basis appears, but $f(A)$ is defined as a polynomial in $A$. This \emph{doesn't} mean that the matrix function itself is a polynomial, i.e. that there exists a $q\in\C\qty[t]$ such that $f(A)=q(A)$ for all $A\in\C{n}{n}$ for which $f(A)$ is defined. In fact, in Definition~\ref{def:matfun_poly}, $p$ depends on $A$.
\end{oss*}

\subsection*{Cauchy integral formula definition}
The function of a matrix can also be defined via a generalization of Cauchy's integral formula $f(z_0)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{\Gamma}\frac{f(z)}{z-z_0}\dd{z}$.
\begin{definizione}\label{def:matfun_integral}
	Let $A\in\C{n}{n}$, let $f$ be analytic on and inside a closed contour $\Gamma$ that encloses $\Lambda(A)\subseteq\C$. Then $f$ can be defined on $A$ as
	\[
		f(A)\coloneqq\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{\Gamma}f(z)(zI-A)^{-1}\dd{z}.
	\]
\end{definizione}
In Definition~\ref{def:matfun_integral}, the integral is defined on a element-by-element basis.  

\section{Basic properties of matrix functions}
Many properties follow from the definition of matrix function. Some of the most useful ones will be listed here, the reader is referred to~\cite[§~1.3]{higham:matfun_book} for further readings. \textcolor{red}{Properties more specific to the matrix exponential will be listed later.}

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:matfun_trivial_properties}
	Let $A\in\C{n}{n}$ and let $f$ be a function defined on the spectrum of $A$. The following properties hold:
	\begin{enumerate}
		\item $Af(A)=f(A)A$
		\item $f(MAM^{-1})=	Mf(A)M^{-1}$
		\item If $A=\operatorname{Blkdiag}\qty(A_{1,1},\dots,A_{m,m})$ is block diagonal, \\
			  then $f(A)=\operatorname{Blkdiag}\qty(f(A_{1,1}),\dots,f(A_{m,m}))$.
		\item $\Lambda(f(A))=\qty{f(\lambda)\mid\lambda\in\Lambda(A)}$, $f(A)$ and $A$ have the same generalized eigenvectors.
	\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
	All the properties are easy consequences of the Jordan canonical form definition, or the polynomial interpolation definition. For a full proof, see~\cite[Thm.~1.13]{higham:matfun_book}, where more properties are listed.
\end{proof}

\begin{oss*}
	The fourth property of Lemma~\ref{lemma:matfun_trivial_properties} estabilishes a close relationship between the eigenvalues of $A$ and those of $f(A)$. In particular, by looking at $f(J_{n_k})$ for each of the Jordan blocks in the canonical form of $A$~\eqref{eqn:jordan_A}, one can see that the algebraic multiplicities are the same, i.e. $\mu_a(\lambda)=\mu_a(f(\lambda))\quad\forall\,\lambda\in\Lambda(A)$.
	
	However, the Jordan structure itself may be radically different. The subspaces' sizes $n_k$ may be different, in particular $\mu_g(\lambda)=\mu_g(f(\lambda))$ does not hold in general.
	
	The full situation is described in~\cite[Thm.~1.36]{higham:matfun_book}, to which the reader is referred for further details.
\end{oss*}

\begin{lemma}
	Let $f$ and $g$ be functions defined on the spectrum of $A\in\C{n}{n}$. The following properties hold:
	\begin{enumerate}
		\item Let $h(z)=f(z)+g(z)$. Then $h(A)=f(A)+g(A)$
		\item Let $h(z)=f(z)\cdot g(z)$. Then $h(A)=f(A)\cdot g(A)$
		\item If $g$ is $n_k-1$ times differentiable on $f(\lambda_k)$ for each $\lambda_k\in\hat{\Lambda}(A)$, then $h=g\circ f$ is defined on the spectrum of $A$, and $h(A) = g(f(A))$
	\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
	The first two properties follow from the polynomial interpolation definition of matrix function, see~\cite[Thm.~1.15]{higham:matfun_book}. The full proof of the third property -- which still follows from the polynomial definition as well, but is slighly more involved -- can be found in~\cite[Thm.~1.17]{higham:matfun_book}.
\end{proof}

The property in Lemma~\ref{lemma:matfun_f_continuity} trivially follows from the Jordan block definition of matrix function, but has important consequences.
\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:matfun_f_continuity}
	Let $\qty{f_m}_{m\in\mathbb{N}}$ be a sequence of functions defined on the spectrum of $A\in\C{n}{n}$, such that, for a function $f$, 
	\[
	\lim_m f_m^{(j)}(\lambda_k)=f^{(j)}(\lambda_k)\quad\forall\,j=0,\dots,n_k\qquad\forall\,\lambda_k\in\hat{\Lambda}(A).
	\]
	Then $\lim_m f_m(A)=f(A)$. 
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
	By definition, $f_m$ is applied block by block to the Jordan form of $A$. Therefore we can assume without loss of generality that the Jordan form of $A$ consists in a single Jordan block $J$; there exists $V\in\C{n}{n}$ such that $A=VJV^{-1}$. Thus,
	\[
	\lim_m f_m(A)=\lim_m f_m(VJV^{-1})=\lim_m Vf_m(J)V^{-1}=V\,\lim_m f_m(J)\,V^{-1}.
	\]
	From~\eqref{eqn:f_Jordan_block} it follows that, under our assumptions, $\lim_m f_m(A)=f(A)$, which completes the proof.
\end{proof}
\begin{corollario_lemma}
	Let $f$ be defined by the power series expansion $f(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}c_k(z-\alpha)^k$ with radius of convergence $r>0$. If $A\in\C{n}{n}$ is such that $\rho(A-\alpha I)<r$ then
	\[
		f(A)=\lim_d\sum_{k=0}^{d}c_k(A-\alpha I)^k.
	\]
\end{corollario_lemma}
The assumption $\rho(A-\alpha I)<r$ can be also loosened to match those of Lemma~\ref{lemma:matfun_f_continuity} when an eigenvalue lies on the border of the disk of convergence. See also~\cite[Thm.~4.7]{higham:matfun_book}.
\smallskip 

\noindent Lemma~\ref{lemma:matfun_A_continuity} states that, under appropriate assumptions (which are satisfied, in the complex case, if the function is holomorphic), matrix functions are continuous functions.
\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:matfun_A_continuity}
	Let $\Omega$ be an open subset of $\R$ or $\C$, let $f$ be $n-1$ times differentiable on $\Omega$. Then $\lim_{m}f(A_m)=f(A)$ for any sequence $\qty{A_m}_{m\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq\qty{A\in\C{n}{n}\mid\Lambda(A)\subseteq\Omega}$ such that $A_m\xlongrightarrow{m\to\infty}A$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
	The proof can be found in~\cite[Thm.~1.19]{higham:matfun_book} and the references therein. Note that the property can't be proven as a straightforward outcome of the polynomial interpolation definition; i.e. $\lim_{m}f(A_k)=\lim_{m}p(A_k)=p(\lim_{m}A_k)$ is \emph{not} a valid proof, because the interpolating polynomial $p$ depends on $A_m$.
\end{proof}


\section{Conditioning of matrix functions}
The general definitions of absolute and relative condition numbers have been given in Definition~\ref{def:cond_number}, in Section~\ref{sect:conditioning_general}. These definitions can also be \textcolor{red}{applied, \emph{mutatis mutandis},} to matrix functions. 

However, in that same section, we discussed how there are explicit formulas for the condition number, for vector valued functions, that involve the norm of the Jacobian matrix. A more practical formula would be welcome even in this case.
To obtain it, a reasonable notion of derivative is needed. The \textcolor{red}{definition of Fréchet derivative is the one we are looking for.}

\begin{definizione}\label{def:frechet_derivative}
	The \emph{Fréchet differential} (often also called \emph{Fréchet derivative}) of  $f\colon\C{n}{n}\to\C{n}{n}$ at the point $A\in\C{n}{n}$ is a linear mapping 
	\[
		\begin{array}{rccl}
			L_f(A)\colon & \C{n}{n} & \longrightarrow & \C{n}{n}\\
					& E & \longmapsto & L_f(A)[E]
		\end{array}
	\]
	such that $f(A+E) = f(A) + L_f(A)[E] + o(\norm{E})$ for all $E\in\C{n}{n}$
\end{definizione}
Definition~\ref{def:frechet_derivative} should be regarded as a specialization of the more general notion of Fréchet differential that can be encountered in real analysis or differential geometry. Nevertheless, it is entirely sufficient for the purposes of this work.

The Fréchet derivative of $f$ at $A$ may not exist, but if it exists then it is unique.
