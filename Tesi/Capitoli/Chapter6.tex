\chapter{Numerical Experiments}\label{capitolo:6}
In this chapter, the numerical experiments to assess and validate the \texttt{expm\_mp} algorithm will be thouroughly described. Then, their outcomes will be presented, emphasizing the most relevant ones. A critical discussion of the obtained results will be given, and conclusions will be drawn.

\section{Experimental setup}
In~\cite{higham:hf19_mpexpm} the proposed algorithm is tested on matrices taken from many sources, most of them having elements that are explicitely specified, such as
\begin{equation}\label{eqn:Higham_test_matrix}
	A = \begin{pmatrix}
		\mathtt{0} & \mathtt{1e{-4}}\\
		\mathtt{-1e{-4}} & \mathtt{0}
	\end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}
The reference solution is computed using \texttt{exp\_mct} -- the \texttt{expm} function provided by the Multiprecision Computing Toolbox -- with $2d$ the significant digits, where $d$ is the number of digits of precision that the algorithm under test is running with. 

For the accuracy analysis of the algorithm proposed in~\cite{exptayotf18}, the authors use matrices of type $B=H^{\top}DH$, where $H$ is a Hadamard matrix normalized in order to be orthogonal (i.e. each entry is divided by $\sqrt{n}$), and $D$ is a diagonal matrix with entries uniformly randomly distributed in $[-50,50] + \mathtt{i}[-50,50]$. 
\smallskip 

This second kind of test looks attractive at first, as the exact exponential can be cheaply and easily computed as $H^{\top}\!\exp(D)H$, without the need to run multiprecision algorithms whose reliability is still being questioned. 
By contrast, one may raise two objections to the first testing strategy. Firstly, the reference is produced by an algorithm that is under scrutiny; secondly, and most importantly, the input data is in double precision and explicitely specified.

How can we then hope to observe an error less than $u_{53}$? Since, as we've seen, promoting numerical expressions doesn't always improve the accuracy, \textcolor{red}{is an error being introduced at the very start}?
Is the accuracy of the computation -- whatever the precision it's being carried with -- limited by that of double precision floating point numbers? 
\smallskip

\textcolor{red}{It turns out that the first testing strategy is ok; and the second one is fine, but introduces a tiny error that doesn't have to be there. The three questions with which this section ends are fallacious. Let's try to make sense of it all}

When one sees $A$ as in~\eqref{eqn:Higham_test_matrix}, one is \emph{biased} to think that it comes from $A = \mathcal{A}+\Delta\mathcal{A}$, where $\mathcal{A}=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}
	0 & 10^{-4}\\ -10^{-4} & 0
\end{smallmatrix}\right)$ is the matrix that was supposed to be fed to the algorithm but can't be because of rounding errors. Therefore, one concludes, $A$ is not an appropriate test matrix for \texttt{exp\_mp} because the algorithm will always fail to compute $\mathcal{Y}\coloneqq\exp(\mathcal{A})$ due to intrinsic error in its input. 
\begin{oss*}
	\textcolor{red}{Of course the elements in $A$ reinforce this bias. But the bias is more general I would say. \`E il discorso che i dati in doppia precisione sono accompagnati dalla percezione di essere ``erronei''.}
\end{oss*}

Let $Y\coloneqq\exp(A)$. The algorithm \texttt{exp\_mp} will produce an approximation $\hat{Y}$.
More formally, the (fallacious) argument is as follows: to correctly assess the quality of our algorithms, $\norm*{\hat{Y}-\mathcal{Y}}/\norm{\mathcal{Y}}$ should be measured. Except in some special cases, $\mathcal{Y}$ will never be gotten, however
\begin{equation}\label{eqn:Y_rel_err_expansion}
	\begin{aligned}
	\frac{\norm*{\hat{Y}-\mathcal{Y}}}{\norm{\mathcal{Y}}} &= \frac{\norm*{\hat{Y}-Y+Y-\mathcal{Y}}}{\norm{\mathcal{Y}}}\\
		&\le\frac{\norm*{\hat{Y}-Y}}{\norm{Y}}\frac{\norm*{Y-\mathcal{Y}+\mathcal{Y}}}{\norm{\mathcal{Y}}}+\frac{\norm{Y-\mathcal{Y}}}{\norm{\mathcal{Y}}}\\
		&\le\frac{\norm*{\hat{Y}-Y}}{\norm{Y}}\left(1+\frac{\norm{Y-\mathcal{Y}}}{\norm{\mathcal{Y}}}\right)+\frac{\norm{Y-\mathcal{Y}}}{\norm{\mathcal{Y}}}\\
		&\dot{=}\frac{\norm*{\hat{Y}-Y}}{\norm{Y}}+\frac{\norm{Y-\mathcal{Y}}}{\norm{\mathcal{Y}}}\le\frac{\norm*{\hat{Y}-Y}}{\norm{Y}}+\kappa_{\exp}(\mathcal{A})\frac{\norm{\Delta\mathcal{A}}}{\norm{\mathcal{A}}}
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where $\dot{=}$ denotes equality up to first order terms (i.e. we dropped higher order terms \textcolor{red}{Dovrebbe avere senso, anche se l'ho fatto forse con troppa leggerezza. Comunque questa non vuol essere una dimostrazione}). A term $\norm{\Delta\mathcal{A}}/\norm{\mathcal{A}}$ appears, with $\norm{\Delta\mathcal{A}}=O(u_{53})$, and is further amplified by $\kappa_{\exp}(\mathcal{A})$. Therefore the forward error can be higher than what we should be able to get, namely something close to $\kappa_{\exp}(A)u_{256}$. The algorithm may be good, but using $A$ we will never be sure. 

\subsubsection*{Addressing the objections}
Behind the fallacy there is the belief that only $\norm*{\mathcal{Y}-\hat{\mathcal{Y}}}/\norm{\mathcal{Y}}$ would be able to capture errors of the order of $u_{256}$, but since we're doomed to use $\hat{Y}$ instead of $\hat{\mathcal{Y}}$ our error reads will be unreliable. 

The \textcolor{red}{steps} in~\eqref{eqn:Y_rel_err_expansion} are correct \textcolor{red}{(o almeno spero!)},  
the point is that although $\exp(A)$ may never be gotten exactly, $Y$ can be pushed to be arbitrarily close to it, just like with any real matrix. Thus, $\norm*{\hat{Y}-Y}/\norm{Y}$ can potentially be of the order of $u_{256}$. This forward error, and not $\norm*{\mathcal{Y}-\hat{\mathcal{Y}}}/\norm{\mathcal{Y}}$, is the appropriate measure for the quality of the approximations that our algorithm computes.

\textcolor{red}{Paragrafo da revisionare (forse togliere, così come le tre domande che vengono risposte).}

Returning to the three questions that introduced the ``fallacious argument'', floating point data usually comes with this notion of being ``flawed'' or ``uncertain'', \textcolor{red}{but really is just a matter of roles}. The \emph{bias} is to think that the available digits in $A$ come from a truncation that $\mathtt{big.()}$ should somehow undo. For all we know, the extra digits returned by the command are correct (whatever ``correct'' means). $A$ is the input to the algorithm, it may be affected by intrinsic error or it may not, but we will never know; the fact that is in double precision doesn't imply that it is. The point is that we don't care. 
In fact, $A$ is better thought of as a real square matrix like any other, although containing double precision data. As such, it's a perfectly valid input to an algorithm running in higher precision. $\mathcal{Y}$ is just a distraction.

\subsection{Why the second testing strategy is (subtly) deceitful}
Let's now consider the second testing strategy, which is summarized in the code snippet~\ref{alg:second_test_strat}.
\begin{algorithm}[H]
	\caption{\emph{Data creation for the second testing strategy}}
	\label{alg:second_test_strat}
	\begin{algorithmic}[1]
		\State $H = \mathtt{hadamard}(n)$
		\State $H = H / \sqrt{n}$
		\State $D = \operatorname{Diag}\qty(100\,\mathtt{rand}(n)\mathtt{.-}50 + 100\mathtt{i}\,\mathtt{rand}(n)\mathtt{.-}50)$
		\State $A = H^{\top}\!\mathtt{*}D\mathtt{*}H$
		\State $Y_{\textup{true}} = H^{\top}\!\mathtt{*}\exp(D)\mathtt{*}H$
	\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

%Similarly to the discussion of the first strategy, identifying what is the input data, which matrix exponential we really want to compute, the crucial issue
\begin{notazione}
	Let either the dot symbol or no symbol at all, as in $A\cdot B$ and $AB$ respectively, denote the product in exact arithmetic. Let the $\mathtt{*}$ symbol denote the same operation in floating point arithmetic.
\end{notazione}

Similarly to the first strategy, there is a bias at play here. We would like to have $A=H^{\top}\!DH$, but by doing $H^{\top}\!\mathtt{*}D\mathtt{*}H$ at line $4$ of algorithm~\ref{alg:second_test_strat} an approximation $\tilde{A}=\mathtt{fl}(A)$ is computed instead.

\begin{oss}\label{oss:A_double_prec}
	The starting data $D$, $H$ is in double precision; $H$ is exactly representable in double precision because $H_{i,j}=\pm 1/\sqrt{n}$ and $n$ is a power of two. 	
	However, $A=H^{\top}DH$ may not belong to the set $\mathcal{F}^{n\times n}$ of matrices with double precision elements. Let $\mathtt{round}(A)\in\mathcal{F}^{n\times n}$ be the closest matrix to $A$.
	
	The best that we can reasonably hope for is $\mathtt{round}(A)$, but line 4 will in general compute something different (see remark~\ref{oss:A_rel_err}). Note that by increasing the precision between lines $3$ and $4$ of algorithm~\ref{alg:second_test_strat} it is possible to obtain $\tilde{A}=\mathtt{round}(A)$\textcolor{red}{, giusto? Guardando~\cite[p.64]{higham:accuracy_book} direi di sì, anche se si riferisce al prodotto scalare.}
\end{oss}

\begin{oss}\label{oss:A_rel_err}
	How close will $A$ and $H^{\top}\!DH$ be, in general? $A$ will be formed in two steps as $K=H^{\top}\!\mathtt{*}D;\ A=K\mathtt{*}H$, and we know that multiplying by $H$ is a backward stable operation. For any unitarily invariant norm we have
	\begin{equation}\label{eqn:matmul_stability}
	\begin{aligned}
		K &= H^{\top}\!\mathtt{*}D=H^{\top}\!(D+\Delta D)&\text{with}\ \norm{\Delta D}\le O(u)\norm{D}\\
		A &= K\mathtt{*}H=(K+\Delta K)H&\text{with}\ \norm{\Delta K}\le O(u)\norm{K}
	\end{aligned}
	\end{equation}
	Now, $A=H^{\top}D\,H+H^{\top}\Delta{D}\,H+\Delta{K}\,H$, hence 
	\begin{align*}
		\norm*{A-H^{\top}\,D\,H} &= \norm*{H^{\top}\Delta{D}\,H + \Delta{K}\,H} = \norm*{H^{\top}\Delta{D} + \Delta{K}}\\
		 &\le\norm{\Delta D} + \norm{\Delta{K}}\le O(u)\qty(\norm{D}+\norm{K})\\
		 &\le O(u)\qty(\norm{D}+\norm{D+\Delta D})\le 3\,O(u)\norm{D}
	\end{align*}
	Note that numerous $O(u)$ factors have been incorporated into a single one, and the constant multiplying $u$ may hide a polynomial factor in $n$ of modest degree. %\textcolor{red}{direi 1. Forse 2. \`E importante?}
	In our case, the bound can be made even be more explicit. For example, $\norm{D}_2\le 50^2$.
\end{oss}
\smallskip

We would like to compare $A$ with $\exp(A)=H^{\top}\!\exp(D)H$. Reasoning as in remark~\ref{oss:A_double_prec}, we must contempt ourselves with $Y_{\textup{true}}$ being an approximation $\widetilde{\exp(A)}$ of $\exp(A)$.
	
The algorithm however is fed with $\tilde{A}$, not $A$, thus it returns and approximation $\hat{Y}$ of $\exponential(\tilde{A})$, not of $\exp(A)$. Therefore $\hat{Y}$ should be compared with a good proxy of $\exponential(\tilde{A})$, not with $Y_{\textup{true}}$.

It may be objected that the previous argument relies on the fact that matrix multiplications are performed, causing the input to the algorithm to be different from $A$. If $\tilde{A}=H^{\top}\mathtt{*}D\mathtt{*}H$ was treated as the starting matrix, similarly to the first strategy, then the argument would not hold.   

This perspective is fallacious as well; if we chose to form $\tilde{A}$ then we would have to forget about $D$ and $H$, thus $H^{\top}\mathtt{*}\exp(D)\mathtt{*}H$ wouldn't be a good approximation of $\exponential{\tilde{A}}$ anyway. 
\smallskip

By readapting the argument used for~\eqref{eqn:matmul_stability},
\[
	\tilde{A} = H^{\top}\mathtt{*}D\mathtt{*}H=H^{\top}\,(D+\Delta{D})\,H\quad\text{with}\ \norm{\Delta{D}}\le O(u_{53})\norm{D}.
\]
By the same argument, there exists a perturbation $\Delta{E}\in\R{n}{n}$, with $\norm{\Delta{E}}\le O(u_{53})\norm{\exp(D)}$, such that
\[
	Y_{\textup{true}} = H^{\top}\mathtt{*}\exp(D)\mathtt{*}H=H^{\top}\,\qty(\exp(D)+\Delta{E})\,H% & \text{with}\ \norm{\Delta{E}}\le O(u_{53})\norm{\exp(D)}
\]
We measure how well $Y_{\textup{true}}$ approximates $\exponential(\tilde{A})$ by means of the forward error. For any unitarily invariant matrix norm,
\begin{align*}
	\norm*{Y_{\textup{true}}-\exponential(\tilde{A})} & = \norm{H^{\top}\,\qty(\exp(D)+\Delta{E})\,H-H^{\top}\,(D+\Delta{D})\,H}\\
	 & = \norm{\exp(D) + \Delta{E} - \exp(D+\Delta{D})} \\
	 & \le \norm{\Delta{E}} + \norm{\exp(D+\Delta{D})-\exp(D)}\\
	 & = O(u_{53})\norm{\exp(D)}+\kappa_{\exp}(D)\frac{\norm{\Delta{D}}}{\norm{D}}\norm{\exp(D)}+O(\norm{D}^2)
\end{align*}
where for this last equality the definition of condition was used (see also~\cite[eq.~(3.3)]{higham:accuracy_book}). Dropping higher order terms and grouping the terms proportional to $u_{53}$ we get
\[
	\norm*{Y_{\textup{true}}-\exponential(\tilde{A})} = O(u_{53})\qty(1+\kappa_{\exp}(D))\norm{\exp(D)}\approx O(u_{53})\norm{L_{\exp}(D)}\norm{D}.
\]
